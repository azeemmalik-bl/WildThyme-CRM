Berlin - January 2015
 

A trip to Berlin to celebrate Niall Shaw's 50th birthday. We arrived into the airport and took a taxi to the Berlin Konzerthaus, as the bar in which we were to meet was virtually next door.

The bar was hideous in a vulgar-nineties-sort-of-way: all flash cash and champagne. The decor was a wall of photos of naked models by Helmut Newton which says it all.

 

The next morning we took a get-on/get-off bus tour round the centre of Berlin, passing most of the major sights, but with an inaudible commentary.

 

In the seats in front of us was a couple with a 'woman' with the longest false eyelashes I have ever seen ...

 
 

Our first stop was at the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Gedächtniskirche (Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church), a Protestant church located on the Kurfürstendamm in the centre of the Breitscheidplatz. It was badly damaged in a bombing raid in 1943. The present building, which consists of a church with an attached foyer and a separate belfry with an attached chapel, was built between 1959 and 1963.

 
The damaged spire of the old church has been retained and its ground floor has been made into a memorial hall which houses the Coventry Cross of Nails - a gift of reconciliation made from nails in the roof timbers of Coventry Cathedral, which had been severely damaged in a German air raid on 14 November 1940. The Memorial Church today is a famous landmark of western Berlin, and is nicknamed by Berliners "der hohle Zahn", meaning "the hollow tooth".
 
 

The Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe (Holocaust Memorial) is a memorial in Berlin to the Jewish victims of the Holocaust, designed by architect Peter Eisenman and engineer Buro Happold. It consists of a 19,000-square-metre site covered with 2,711 concrete slabs or stelae, arranged in a grid pattern on a sloping field. The original plan was to place nearly 4,000 slabs, but before the unveiling a new law was passed mandating memorials to be wheelchair accessible. After the recalculation, the number of slabs that could legally fit into the designated areas was 2,711.

After lunch we visited the Deutsche Kinemathek - a museum of film and television which was immersive and totally fascinating and included an exhibition of costumes worn by Marlene Dietrich as well as tableaux inside film studios and filming techniques.

 
 

Then onto the Jüdisches Museum which opened in 2001 and is the largest Jewish museum in Europe. It consists of three buildings, two of which are new additions specifically built for the museum by architect Daniel Libeskind following a competition. While other entrants proposed cool, neutral spaces, Libeskind offered a radical, zigzag design, which earned the nickname "Blitz" (Lightning) and which can be seen on the left in the aerial picture below.

 

Israeli artist Menashe Kadishman created the installation Shalekhet – Fallen leaves, 10,000 faces punched out of steel and distributed on the ground of the Memory Void, the only "voided" space of the Libeskind Building that can be entered. Kadishman dedicated his artwork not only to Jews killed during the Shoah, but to all victims of violence and war. Visitors are invited to walk on the faces and listen to the sounds created by the metal sheets, as they clang and rattle against one another.

This Torah-writing robot, then on display in the Jewish Museum, was developed by the German artists' group 'robotlab'. While it takes the machine about three months to complete the 260-foot-long scroll, a rabbi or a sofer - a Jewish scribe - needs nearly a year. In order for the Torah to be holy, it has to be written with a goose feather on parchment. But, unlike the rabbi's work, the robot's Torah can't be used in a synagogue.
 
Geraldine (Niall's wife) had really done her research on Berlin and found some extraordinary off-the-tourist-trail places for us to visit, including a modern bar on the tenth floor of a block of flats.
 
Opened in 1894, Berlin's Reichstag housed the German Parliament until 1933, when it was severely damaged after being set on fire. After World War II, the building fell into disuse; the parliament of the German Democratic Republic (the Volkskammer) met in the Palast der Republik in East Berlin, while the parliament of the Federal Republic of Germany (the Bundestag) met in the Bundeshaus in Bonn. The ruined building was made safe against the elements and partially refurbished in the 1960s, but no attempt at full restoration was made until after German reunification in 1990, when it underwent a reconstruction led by architect Norman Foster. After its completion in 1999, it once again became the meeting place of the German parliament: the modern Bundestag.
 
 
 
And, finally, a visit to the Komische Oper for a performance of Offenbach's La belle Hélène directed in truly delicious camp style by Barrie Kosky.
