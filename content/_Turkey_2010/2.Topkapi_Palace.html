Istanbul - Topkapi Palace

.

Between 1459 and 1465, shortly after his conquest of Constantinople, Mehmet II built Topkapi Palace as his principal residence. Rather than a single building, it was conceived as a series of pavilions contained by four enormous courtyards, a stone version of the tented encampments from which the nomadic Ottomans had emerged. 

In the Divan (council chamber), the viziers of the imperial council met, sometimes watched covertly by the Sultan. 

 

 

 

Apart from the Sultan's mother and the Sultan's daughters, the most powerful women in the Harem were slaves, gathered from the far corners of the Ottoman Empire. Their dream was to become the favourite of the Sultan and bear him a son which, on some occasions, led to marriage. Competition was stiff and, at its height, the Harem contained over 1,000 concubines. The last women eventually left the Harem in 1909. Exquisitely tiled in blue and gold, these quarters appeared tranquil and calm despite the emotions raging beneath. 

 

The Baghdad Pavilion was built in 1639 by Murat IV to celebrate his capture of Baghdad. 

In front of it stands the Iftar Gazebo. Built in 1640 during the reign of Sultan Ibrahim, the structure has gold tulip-shaped roof and is supported by four gold-plated copper columns. In addition to its use for holiday festivities, the gazebo was also the place where sultans would break their fast in the evening (Iftar) whenever the month of Ramadan fell in the summertime. 

 

Archeological restoration still continues at the Palace. This appeared to be a repository of bits of masonry which could be fitted back together. 

A misty view back over the Bosphorus. 

The old kitchens, with their tall chimneys, now contain an exhibition of ceramics, glass and silverware.
