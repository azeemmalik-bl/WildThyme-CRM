1931
The Bad Sister
Laura Madison
Hobart Henley
The film marks the screen debut of Bette Davis and also included Humphrey Bogart in a supporting role. Davis and her mother attended a preview of the film in San Bernardino, California and Davis was reportedly so distressed by her performance that they left before the final credits. Certain her Hollywood career was over, she cried all the way home.
1931
Seed
Margaret Carter
John M. Stahl
Director John M. Stahl cast Bette Davis as Margaret Carter after seeing her in the studio commissary. It proved to be the smallest role of her career, and in later years she recalled "If you blinked for a moment, you would have missed me. I should have joined the extra's union."
1931
Waterloo Bridge
Janet Cronin
James Whale
In the cast was Bette Davis who, in the small role of Janet Cronin, was listed sixth out of nine in the opening credits and was ignored by all the critics. "Perfectly logical," Davis later recalled. "I had about four lines ... I yearned all during shooting to play Myra (the leading role). I could have!"
1931
Way Back Home
Mary Lucy Duffy
William A. Seiter
RKO producer Pandro S. Berman negotiated with Universal for the loan of Bette Davis, whose contract with the studio had been renewed following her completion of Waterloo Bridge, for a fee of $300 per week. Davis was pleased with the attention paid to her by cinematographer J. Roy Hunt. "I was truly overjoyed," she later recalled. "Plus the part I played was an important one - and a charming one. It gave me some confidence in myself for the first time."
1932
The Menace
Peggy Lowell
Roy William Neill
Shot in only eight days, Bette Davis was loaned to Columbia for the small supporting role of Peggy Lowell. Also in the cast was Murray Kinnell, who recommended Davis to his close friend George Arliss who was searching for an actress for the ingenue role in The Man Who Played God, which generally is acknowledged as the film that finally brought the actress to the attention of critics and the movie-going public.
1932
Hell's House
Peggy Gardner
Howard Higgin
The film, shot in thirteen days, originally was entitled Juvenile Court. Bette Davis was loaned to B. F. Zeidman Productions Ltd. by Universal, and following her completion of this film studio head Carl Laemmle, Jr. allowed her option to drop. She was preparing to return to New York City when George Arliss offered her the ingenue role in The Man Who Played God.
1932
The Man Who Played God
Grace Blair
John G. Adolfi
George Arliss stars as a concert pianist embittered by the loss of his hearing who eventually finds redemption in helping others; it also features a then little-known Bette Davis as the much younger woman in love with the protagonist. The review in The Hollywood Reporter was entitled "Clean, Wholesome, and Dull".
1932
So Big!
Miss Dallas O'Mara
William A. Wellman
Davis' second film for Warner Bros., and the first in which she appeared with George Brent, who co-starred with her in eleven more films. She considered her casting in a prestigious Barbara Stanwyck project a sign Jack L. Warner was acknowledging her value to the studio. In her autobiography A Lonely Life (1962), she recalled, "It was a source of tremendous satisfaction, and encouraged me to unheard-of dreams of glory."
1932
The Rich Are Always with Us
Malbro
Alfred E. Green
A Bette Davis, cast in the supporting role of Malbro, filmed The Rich Are Always with Us simultaneously with So Big!, which was released first. It was the first time she was photographed by Ernest Haller who became her favourite cinematographer - she referred to him as "the genius" and "my miracle man" - and he worked with her on thirteen additional projects. Davis was a longtime fan of leading lady Ruth Chatterton and looked forward to co-starring with her.
1932
The Dark Horse
Kay Russell
Alfred E. Green
A jailbird is hired to lead a dimwitted candidate's campaign for governor. Bette Davis is rather wasted in this film - she plays Warren William's loyal and efficient secretary who loves him and does her best with a traditional good girl role, but there isn't much for her to work with. Vivienne Osborne gives the ex-wife part the kind of bite we normally find in Bette Davis films later on.
1932
The Cabin in the Cotton
Madge Norwood
Michael Curtiz
A 1932 American pre-Code drama film perhaps best known for a line of dialogue spoken by a platinum-blonde Bette Davis in a Southern drawl - "I'd like ta kiss ya, but I just washed my hair." - a line lifted directly from the book. In later years it was immortalised by Davis impersonators and quoted in the 1995 film Get Shorty.
1932
Three on a Match
Ruth Wescott
Mervyn LeRoy

Three women who went to the same elementary school, Mary (Joan Blondell), Ruth (Bette Davis), and Vivian (Ann Dvorak), meet again as young adults after some time apart. They each light a cigarette from the same match and discuss the superstition that such an act is unlucky and that Vivian, the last to light her cigarette, will be the first to die.

1932
20,000 Years in Sing Sing
Fay Wilson
Michael Curtiz

Set in Sing Sing Penitentiary, the maximum security prison in New York, and starring Spencer Tracy as an inmate and Bette Davis as his girlfriend. Davis idolised Spencer Tracy, and the two wanted to do another movie together but never had the opportunity to do so, although they did appear together again on a radio version of Dark Victory in 1940.

1933
Parachute Jumper
Patricia "Alabama" Brent
Alfred E. Green
Although sharing billing with Douglas Fairbanks Jr,, Bette Davis later rated Parachute Jumper "dead last" of her films. She saw her character as another in a long line of insignificant roles that were not advancing her career, and she complained to Jack L. Warner about her assigned projects. Clips were featured in the prologue of What Ever Happened to Baby Jane? (1962) as an example of the supposedly poor quality of the film work of Jane Hudson (Bette Davis) as an adult.
1933
The Working Man
Jenny Harland
John G. Adolfi
This Warner Bros. release marked the second pairing of George Arliss and Bette Davis, who co-starred in The Man Who Played God the year before. The New York Times review reported "Bette Davis, whose diction is music to the ears, does good work in the role of Jenny."
1933
Ex-Lady
Helen Bauer
Robert Florey
A remake of the Barbara Stanwyck vehicle Illicit, released two years earlier. A review in TV Guide called it a "lame little melodrama notable chiefly for being the first film to have Bette Davis' name above the title". Davis expressed her disdain for the film calling it a “piece of junk”. She also stated that the film “was supposed to be provocative ... and provoked anyone of sensibility to nausea”.
1933
Bureau of Missing Persons
Norma Roberts
Roy Del Ruth
The second on-screen pairing of Davis and Pat O'Brien, both under contract to Warner Bros., who had appeared Hell's House the previous year. In 1936, the picture was reissued with the opening credits reconfigured to give top billing to Davis, who was by then the studio's leading female star, although her headlining part is relatively small.
1934
The Big Shakedown
Norma Nelson
John Francis Dillon
Jimmy Morrell (Charles Farrell) and Norma Nelson (Bette Davis) who plan to wed as soon as their neighbourhood pharmacy begins to show a profit. However, racketeers flood the market with counterfeit cosmetics and drugs, causing some tragedies. Critics called it an "overblown crime melodrama" and "the material stretches believability at every plot turn".
1934
Fashions of 1934
Lynn Mason
William Dieterle
Jack L. Warner tried to change Bette Davis' screen persona by putting her in a platinum blonde wig and false eyelashes and dressing her in glamorous costumes. The actress, who had been trying to convince the studio head to loan her to RKO so she could portray slatternly waitress Mildred Rogers in Of Human Bondage, was appalled at the transformation, complaining they were trying to turn her into Greta Garbo. With musical numbers created and directed by Busby Berkeley.
1934
Jimmy the Gent
Miss Joan Martin
Michael Curtiz
An unpolished racketeer, whose racket is finding heirs for unclaimed fortunes, affects ethics and tea-drinking manners to win back the sweetheart who now works for his seemingly upright competitor. It was the first pairing of Cagney and Davis, who would reunite for The Bride Came C.O.D. seven years later.
1934
Fog Over Frisco
Arlene Bradford
William Dieterle
Bette Davis, anxious to portray the slatternly waitress Mildred in the RKO Radio Pictures production Of Human Bondage, accepted the relatively small role of Arlene in the hope her cooperation would convince Jack L. Warner to lend her to the rival studio for the film. Her ploy worked, and when Warner received word about her dynamic performance in Bondage, he elevated her to top billing in Fog Over Frisco.
1934
Of Human Bondage
Mildred Rogers
John Cromwell
Widely regarded by critics as the film that made Bette Davis a star. She designed her own make-up for the scenes depicting Mildred's illness, changed from syphilis to tuberculosis to satisfy the demands of the Hays Code - "I made it very clear that Mildred was not going to die of a dread disease looking as if a deb had missed her noon nap." A reviewer in Life magazine called her performance the greatest ever recorded on screen by an actress. Her first Oscar-nomination for Best Actress but losing to Claudette Colbert for It Happened One Night.
1934
Housewife
Patricia "Pat" Berkeley
Alfred E. Green
The New York Times observed, "A characteristic of a poor boxer is that he telegraphs his punches. In Housewife ... the dramatic punches are not merely telegraphed, but radioed." Although Variety called it "satisfactory entertainment" when asked about the film in later years, Bette Davis responded, "Dear God! What a horror!"
1935
Bordertown
Mrs Marie Roark
Archie Mayo
In Mexico, a former lawyer becomes the invaluable second-in-command to a casino owner, whose wife begins to fall for him. One of the first films to come under the close scrutiny of the Hays Office. "The part of Marie was an excellent acting part - a step in the direction of where I wanted my career to go", Davis later recalled. "I wanted to be known as an actress, not necessarily a star, although that would be the frosting on the cake if it should ever come about".
1935
The Girl from 10th Avenue
Miriam A. Brady
Alfred E. Green
A lawyer impulsively marries a stranger after his fiancée dumps him.Variety said the film "is fashioned from a pattern whose every turn and twist the dullest fan can easily anticipate ... narrative is chockful of implausible sequences and the plot ... often gets itself into blind alleys. But deft direction plus smooth trouping by Davis make these defects not too noticeable."
1935
Front Page Woman
Ellen Garfield
Michael Curtiz
A woman reporter tries to prove she's just as good as any man, but runs into trouble along the way. This Warner Bros. release was one of three 1935 films co-starring Bette Davis and George Brent. The two were paired on-screen a total of thirteen times. This was the fourth collaboration for Davis and director Michael Curtiz. The two worked together a total of seven times.
1935
Special Agent
Julie Gardner
William Keighley
Special Agent was one of three 1935 films co-starring Bette Davis and George Brent. Neither was happy with the finished product. Brent told Photoplay that the picture was "a poor, paltry thing, unbelievable and unconvincing." The Hays office objected to a bit of dialogue by one character so, rather than reshoot the scene, the sound was eliminated. The actor's lips can be seen moving but there was no sound.
1935
Dangerous
Joyce Heath
Alfred E. Green
Winning Bette Davis her first Best Actress Oscar, she initially turned down the script, but Warner Bros. producer Hal B. Wallis convinced her she could make something special out of the character, She was determined to look like an actress on the skids, and insisted Orry-Kelly design costumes appropriate for a woman who had seen better days. It was for this film Perc Westmore styled her hair in the bob cut she would favor for the rest of her life.
1936
The Petrified Forest
Gabrielle "Gabby" Maple
Archie Mayo
A waitress, a hobo and a bank robber get mixed up at a lonely diner in the desert. Film version of the Broadway stage play which had also starred Leslie Howard and Humphrey Bogart. The movie was a breakthrough for Bogart, though it typecast him as a heavy. Davis and Howard had teamed up two years earlier in Of Human Bondage and the chemistry between them was important to making this quirky Western plausible.
1936
The Golden Arrow
Daisy Appleby
Alfred E. Green
Although audience reaction to the film at a preview in Long Beach, California was dismal, Warner Bros. rushed it into release to capitalise on the Oscar Award Davis had recently won for Dangerous. She was upset with the publicity for the film which she thought was ridiculous. "The was the beginning of the end ... of my contract with Warner Bros.," she later recalled. "I was actually insulted to have to appear in such a cheap, nothing story after Of Human Bondage, The Petrified Forest and Bordertown."
1936
Satan Met a Lady
Valerie Purvis
William Dieterle
A loose adaptation of the 1929 novel The Maltese Falcon. Bette Davis, upset that she was being forced to film "junk" after completing a prestige project like The Petrified Forest, failed to report to the set and was suspended by Jack L. Warner. However, angry and resentful but in need of her salary to cover living expenses for her mother and medical care for her sister, she reported to work three days later.
1937
Marked Woman
Mary Dwight Strauber
Lloyd Bacon
Set in the underworld of Manhattan, Marked Woman tells the story of a woman who dares to stand up to one of the city's most powerful gangsters. Co-starring Humphrey Bogart, the film was a major success for Warner Brothers to whom Davis had recently lost a lawsuit about the inferior quality of scripts she was expected to play. She gained considerable press coverage, and this film was the first script she filmed upon returning to Hollywood: she was reported to be very pleased with the script, and the dramatic possibilities it afforded her.
1937
Kid Galahad
Louise "Fluff" Phillips
Michael Curtiz
Fight promoter Nick Donati grooms a bellhop as a future champ, but has second thoughts when the 'kid' falls for his sister. Also starring Edward G. Robinson and Humphrey Bogart. Bette Davis "doesn't have the kind of role that is really up to her talents, but she still turns in a powerful performance, using those famous eyes of hers to speak volumes". It was remade in 1941, this time in a circus setting, as The Wagons Roll at Night, also with Bogart, and in 1962 as an Elvis Presley musical.
1937
That Certain Woman
Mary Donnell
Edmund Goulding
Of the film, Bette Davis said it "was certainly not one of my favorite scripts. There was a falseness to the whole project. But I did meet and work with Edmund Goulding for the first time. He concentrated on attractive shots of me, in other words, gave me the star treatment. It was the first time I had this. I was always a member of the cast - a leading member - but not made special in the way Goulding made me special in this film." Davis and Goulding went on to collaborate on three more films: Dark Victory (1939), The Old Maid (1939) and The Great Lie (1941).
1937
It's Love I'm After
Joyce Arden
Archie Mayo
Screwball comedy co- starring Leslie Howard and Olivia de Havilland. The film began production without a leading lady. Then producer Hal B. Wallis decided the screwball comedy would be a refreshing change-of-pace for Bette Davis. The film is about a couple who have postponed their marriage eleven times and who continue to plot and scheme their way to marriage. "Fast-moving and thoroughly enjoyable."
1938
Jezebel
Julie Marsden
William Wyler
Winning Davis her second Best Actress Oscar, Film Daily said it was "a really outstanding screen triumph for Bette Davis. She plays an emotional role that calls for running the gamut of emotions, and she handles the part with consummate artistry". The film was allegedly offered as compensation for her failure to win the part of Scarlett O'Hara in Gone with the Wind as David O. Selznick never seriously considered her for the role despite a radio poll showing Davis the audience favourite.
1938
The Sisters
Louise Elliott Medlin
Anatole Litvak

Three daughters of a small town pharmacist undergo trials and tribulations in their problematic marriages. Davis was furious to be offered a "junk film" called Comet Over Broadway after her Oscar success and was replaced by Kay Francis. After a truce she accepted this film when Irene Dunne withdrew. She was pleased to co-star with Errol Flynn ("he was a big box office star at the time and it could only be beneficial to me to work with him") but battled with the Studio over having to take second billing to him.

1939
Dark Victory
Judith Traherne
Edmund Goulding
A young socialite is diagnosed with an inoperable brain tumour, and must decide whether or not she'll meet her final days with dignity. Davis had recently ended affairs with William Wyler and Howard Hughes and her husband had filed for divorce, and after the first few days of filming she begged to be released from her contract, claiming she was too sick to continue. Producer Hal Wallis responded, "I've seen the rushes – stay sick!" Oscar-nominated but Davis lost out to Vivien Leigh for Gone With The Wind.
1939
Juarez
Empress Carlotta
William Dieterle
Bette Davis was offered the role of Carlotta while in the midst of filming Dark Victory. Although the part was small, she welcomed the opportunity to portray an historical figure, especially one who would go mad during a dramatic confrontation with Napoléon III. Filming had to be suspended for several days as Davis went down with a bout of pleurisy, possibly caused by stress caused by her divorce. Reviews were generally poor.
1939
The Old Maid
Charlotte Lovell
Edmund Goulding
The arrival of an ex-lover on a young woman's wedding day sets in motion a chain of events which will alter her and her cousin's lives forever. Adapted from an Edith Wharton novella, this was the first film in which Davis had equal screen time with a female co-star, Miriam Hopkins, whom she felt did everything she could to undermine her performance since she resented Davis' success in Jezebel. This included altering her make-up to make her look younger than Davis.
1939
The Private Lives of Elizabeth and Essex
Queen Elizabeth
Michael Curtiz
A depiction of the love/hate relationship between Queen Elizabeth I (Bette Davis) and Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex. (Errol Flynn). Also starring Olivia de Havilland as Lady Penelope Gray and Vincent Price as Sir Walter Raleigh. Bette Davis had lobbied for Laurence Olivier to play the part of Essex but he was then unknown in America. She was tipped to receive an Oscar nomination for her role as Elizabeth, but was nominated for Dark Victory (also from Warner) instead.
1940
All This, and Heaven Too
Henriette Deluzy-Desportes
Anatole Litvak
A duchess' irrational behaviour toward the governess of her children triggers tragic events that will change her family's lives forever. The film was positively reviewed by critics: Variety called it "film theatre at its best ..." but because of its overlong 143 minutes and grim story line, it was not the blockbuster the studio hoped for.
1940
The Letter
Leslie Crosbie
William Wyler
The wife of a rubber plantation administrator shoots a man to death and claims it was self-defense, but a letter in her own hand may prove her undoing. The story was inspired by a real-life scandal involving the wife of  a headmaster in Kuala Lumpur who was convicted in a murder trial after shooting dead a male friend in April 1911. She was eventually pardoned. However due to the Hays Code, and as the original ending contained adultery and unpunished murder, a new final scene was added in which Leslie (the character that Davis plays) is killed. Davis was nominated for Best Actress Oscar but lost out to Ginger Rogers in Kitty Foyle.
1941
The Great Lie
Maggie Patterson
Edmund Goulding

After completing The Letter, Bette Davis was offered the role of Maggie Patterson. "I wasn't very excited about it" she later recalled, but fan mail urging her to play a nice role for a change of pace prompted her to accept. "Maggie was one of the few times I played a character basically like myself off the screen". The role of Sandra Kovak proved difficult to cast. Davis, who enjoyed working with powerful actresses capable of challenging her in scenes in which they interacted, felt Mary Astor (also an accomplished pianist) was ideal, and after a delay, she was offered the role.

1941
Shining Victory
Nurse
Irving Rapper
In a Scottish sanitarium, a brilliant research psychiatrist works on a treatment for dementia precox. He falls for his altruistic female lab assistant and they begin a passionate tragic relationship. Based on the play Jupiter Laughs by A. J. Cronin and starring James Stephenson, Geraldine Fitzgerald, Donald Crisp and Barbara O'Neil. Bette Davis makes a brief cameo appearance as a nurse in the film as a favour to her friend Irving Rapper, whose first film this was.
1941
The Bride Came C.O.D.
Joan Winfield
William Keighley
Screwball romantic comedy starring James Cagney as an aeroplane pilot and Bette Davis as a runaway heiress. Although publicised as the first screen pairing of Warner Bros.' two biggest stars, they had actually made Jimmy the Gent together in 1934, and had wanted to find another opportunity to work together. Principal photography took place in the Death Valley, California in January 1941, and was problematic as temperatures soared, the script problems were unresolved and Davis fell into a cactus, having 45 quills pulled out of her rear.
1941
The Little Foxes
Regina Giddens
William Wyler
The ruthless, moneyed Hubbard clan lives in, and poisons, their part of the deep South at the turn of the twentieth century. Tallulah Bankhead had received critical acclaim for her performance in the 1939 Broadway production of the play, but director William Wyler, who previously had teamed with Bette Davis on Jezebel and The Letter, insisted on casting her instead. Producer Samuel Goldwyn agreed, since none of Bankhead's films had been box office hits. Davis was nominated for Best Actress Oscar but lost out to Joan Fontaine in Suspicion.
1942
The Man Who Came to Dinner
Maggie Cutler
William Keighley
When Bette Davis saw the Broadway production of the play, she decided the role of Maggie Cutler would be a refreshing change of pace following her heavily dramatic role in The Little Foxes. She urged Jack L. Warner to cast John Barrymore opposite her but he was deemed unsuitable when, as a result of his heavy drinking (or perhaps encroaching Alzheimers), he supposedly had difficulty delivering the complicated, fast-paced dialogue. She was unhappy with the casting of Monty Woolley although the film was a great success.
1942
In This Our Life
Stanley Timberlake Kingsmill
John Huston
Bette Davis and Olivia de Havilland star as sisters and rivals in romance in the third of six films that they worked on together. Completed in 1942, the film was forbidden for foreign release in 1943 by the wartime Office of Censorship, because it dealt truthfully with racial discrimination as part of its plot.Three days following the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, Huston left the production for an assignment with the United States Department of War. The film was completed by Raoul Walsh (uncredited) who clashed with Davis on virtually every aspect of the film.
1942
Now, Voyager
Charlotte Vale
Irving Rapper
A frumpy spinster blossoms under therapy and becomes an elegant, independent woman. The initial choices for Charlotte were Irene Dunne, Norma Shearer and Ginger Rogers. When Davis learned about the project, she campaigned for and won the role. Consulting with designer Orry-Kelly, she suggested a drab outfit, including an ugly foulard dress for Charlotte initially, to contrast with the stylish, "timeless" creations that mark her later appearance on the cruise ship. Davis was nominated for Best Actress Oscar but lost out to Greer Garson in Mrs. Miniver.
1943
Watch on the Rhine
Sara Muller
Herman Shumlin
A German-born engineer, his American wife and their children travel from Mexico to the United States to visit her family but their plans are complicated by a Romanian count. Davis stated in a 1971 interview that she played the role of the wife for 'name value' because the studio did not consider the film a good financial risk and that her name above the credits would draw audiences. Davis gladly took the secondary role because she felt the story was so important (she was a fierce opponent of the Nazi party) and that Lillian Hellman's original play was "super brilliant".
1943
Thank Your Lucky Stars
Herself
David Butler

A musical comedy film made by Warner Brothers as a World War II fundraiser, with a slim plot. The stars (Eddie Cantor, Dennis Morgan, Joan Leslie, Edward Everett Horton and S. Z. Sakall) donated their salaries to the Hollywood Canteen, which was founded by Bette Davis and John Garfield. Davis sings "They're Either Too Young or Too Old" in a nightclub set populated by men whose appearances fit the song. A brief and strenuous jitterbug performance by Davis and real-life dance contest winner Conrad Weidel sends her out to her car.

1943
Old Acquaintance
Kit Marlowe
Vincent Sherman
Old friends Kit Marlowe and Millie Drake adopt contrasting lifestyles: Kit is a single, critically acclaimed author while married Millie writes popular pulp novels. Bette Davis initially wanted Norma Shearer to take the role of Millie. But Shearer, who was semi-retired, declined to take the secondary role and second billing. However, Miriam Hopkins who had filmed The Old Maid and endured a difficult working relationship with Davis (due to the resentment created when Davis allegedly had an affair with Hopkins' husband Anatole Litvak) accepted the role.
1944
Mr. Skeffington
Fanny Trellis
Vincent Sherman
A beautiful woman whose many suitors, and self-love, distract her from returning the affections of her husband Job Skeffington. It also makes a point about Skeffington's status as a Jew in 1914 high society, and later, in relation to Nazi Germany. Davis was going through incredible personal torments at this time, which was reflected in her poor treatment of her co-stars on this film. Even Bette Davis is quoted as saying "Only a mother could have loved me at this point in my life." Davis was nominated for Best Actress Oscar but lost out to Ingrid Bergman in Gaslight.
1944
Hollywood Canteen
Herself
Delmer Daves
Two soldiers on leave spend three nights at the Hollywood Canteen before returning to active duty in the South Pacific. Slim Green (Robert Hutton) is the millionth G.I. to enjoy the Canteen, and consequently wins a date with Joan Leslie. The other G.I., Sergeant Nolan (Dane Clark) gets to dance with Joan Crawford. Canteen founders Bette Davis and John Garfield give talks on the history of the Canteen. The soldiers enjoy a variety of musical numbers performed by a host of Hollywood stars, and also comedians, such as Jack Benny.
1945
The Corn Is Green
Miss Lilly Moffat
Irving Rapper
A schoolteacher is determined to bring education to a Welsh coal mining town despite great opposition.and becomes the mentor of a talented young man and seeks to get him into a university. The character  of Miss Moffat was supposed to be in her 50s so, to help her look the part, the 36-year-old Davis wore a grey wig and a "fat suit" that added 30 pounds (14 kg). Later remade as a TV movie with Katharine Hepburn.
1946
A Stolen Life
Kate Bosworth / Patricia Bosworth
Curtis Bernhardt
When a woman's twin sister is drowned, she assumes her identity in order to be close to the man she feels her sister took from her years before. Director Bernhardt had made films in Germany from 1925 until 1933, when he was forced to flee the Nazi regime - who briefly had him arrested - because he was Jewish. The film was nominated for Best Special Effects at the 19th Academy Awards, but lost to Blithe Spirit. The second time Davis played twin sisters was in Dead Ringer (1964).
1946
Deception
Christine Radcliffe
Irving Rapper
A piano teacher believes that her fiancé was killed on the battlefield. When he miraculously returns, they decide to marry, but are threatened by a wealthy, egotistical composer the piano teacher started dating on the rebound after she became convinced her lover had died. The fourth of five films Davis made with Irving Rapper but, despite generally positive reviews, it was the first Bette Davis head-lining movie to lose money for Warner Bros. She discovered that she was pregnant during filming.
1948
Winter Meeting
Susan Grieve
Bretaigne Windust
Spinster poetess Susan Grieve lives in a Manhattan apartment where naval hero Slick Novak comes with her for a nightcap. Next morning they visit her Connecticut farm where Novak tells her he always wanted to be a priest. Will Susan or God win his ultimate love?  Overblown with endless lines of completely tedious dialogue, it was Davis' second film to lose money. Deception, made in 1946, was her first financial failure. As a result, Jack L. Warner lost confidence in Davis as an asset to the studio.
1948
June Bride
Linda Gilman
Bretaigne Windust
Her previous two pictures having been box-office disappointments, this was the first comedy Davis had made since 1941 when she starred in two: The Bride Came C.O.D. and The Man Who Came to Dinner. As a result, Davis was given a new, four-picture contract with a salary of $10,285 a week, making her the highest-paid woman in the United States at the time.Time wrote "Thanks largely to some bright dialogue and an artful performance by Robert Montgomery, this is the best Bette Davis picture in some time."
1949
Beyond the Forest
Rosa Moline
King Vidor
Resentful of her small-town life, a married woman schemes to run off with a rich businessman. The film marks Davis' last appearance as a contract actress for Warner, after eighteen years with the studio. She tried several times to walk away from the film but Warner refused to release her from their employment contract. She remembered the project as "a terrible movie" and her death scene at end in the film as "the longest death scene ever seen on the screen". Described in the book, The Official Razzie Guide as one of the "100 Most Enjoyably Bad Movies Ever Made".
1950
All About Eve
Margo Channing
Joseph L. Mankiewicz
A highly regarded but aging Broadway star is threatened by Anne Baxter who plays Eve Harrington, an ambitious young fan who manoeuvers herself into the star's life, ultimately threatening her career and her personal relationships. The film co-stars George Sanders, Celeste Holm, and features Gary Merrill, Hugh Marlowe, Thelma Ritter and Marilyn Monroe in one of her earliest roles, Features the classic line, "Fasten your seatbelts, it's going to be a bumpy night." Davis was nominated for Best Actress Oscar but lost out to Judy Holliday in Born Yesterday.
1951
Payment on Demand
Joyce Ramsey
Curtis Bernhardt
Chronicling a marriage from its idealistic early days to its dissolution, the film's original title was The Story of a Divorce. It was made in 1949 but was not released until two years later, following the success of All About Eve. Howard Hughes, unhappy with the ending and the title, insisted on a reshoot of the final scene which was spliced into the final reel, arriving at the premiere after the film already had begun under its original title. The projectionist had just enough time to thread his machine with the new final reel. Davis later said, "The new ending broke our hearts. The one we had shot was the true ending for our film. We also were brokenhearted over the title change."
1951
Another Man's Poison
Janet Frobisher
Irving Rapper
A mystery writer becomes involved in a tangled web of murderous deception not unlike the plots of her novels. This was the second on-screen pairing of then-married couple Davis and Gary Merrill, following All About Eve the previous year. They made Phone Call from a Stranger the following year. Rapper, who was selected by Davis to helm the film, had directed her in Now, Voyager ten years earlier.
