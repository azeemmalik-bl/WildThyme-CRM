Tzotzil Villages
Possibly the most extraordinary church I’ve visited, the Templo de San Juan in the Tzotzil village of San Juan Chamula where all face-on photographs of the people are forbidden as they believe that photographs steal the soul. The church has a vividly painted arch of various shades of green but inside there are thousands of flickering candles on altars, tables and across the pine-needled covered floor (surely a fire risk methinks?). Figures of saints are dressed in garish holy garments and surrounded with mirrors, clouds of incense fill the air and kneeling worshippers prostrate themselves on the floor while others drink fizzy drinks or ‘pox’ (alcohol made from sugar cane) as burping is believed to expel evil spirits ...
   
Nearby, around the shell of an older church, is the village graveyard where, traditionally, black crosses are for people who died old, white for the young and blue for others.
Then we travelled on to the Zapatista village of San Lorenzo Zinacantán where we were allowed to enter private houses.
 
 
Zinacantán people, like Chamulans, are Tzotzil. The men wear distinctive pink tunics embroidered with flower motifs and sport flat, round, ribboned palm hats while the women wear pink or purple shawls over richly embroidered blouses.
In many of the houses, stalls had been set up where beautiful embroidery was for sale.
 
 
Two of our fellow travellers (Mary and Kenny from Wimbledon) entered into the spirit and tried on some Tzotzil outfits.
 
While the embroidery and items for sale are beautiful, the living conditions in which these people have to survive are pretty grim and it is no wonder that they have little respect for the Mexican government.
The Iglesia de San Lorenzo was rebuilt following a fire in 1975 and today there are masses of flowers on the altar. Photography is banned in the church and the churchyard - but I only discovered this after our visit ...
  
 
 

The Zapatista Army of National Liberation "EZLN" (often referred to as the Zapatistas) is a far-left libertarian-socialist political and militant group that controls a substantial amount of territory in Chiapas, the southernmost state of Mexico. Since 1994 the group has been nominally at war with the Mexican state, pledging to overturn the oligarchy's centuries-old hold on land, resources and power and improve the wretched living standards of Mexico's indigenous people.

The group takes its name from Emiliano Zapata, the agrarian revolutionary and commander of the Liberation Army of the South during the Mexican Revolution, and sees itself as his ideological heir. Nearly all EZLN villages contain murals with images of Zapata and Ernesto "Che" Guevara.

These photographs were sold as postcards in the same shop as the beautiful embroidery.
