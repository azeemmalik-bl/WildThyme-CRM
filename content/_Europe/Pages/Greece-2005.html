Greece - October 2005
 

After a flight and an overnight in Athens with our friends Jenny and Marilyn, we collected our hire car and drove on down to the Peloponnese over the Corinth Canal which connects the Ionian Sea with the Aegean Sea. It cuts through the narrow Isthmus of Corinth and separates the Peloponnese from the Greek mainland, arguably making the peninsula an island. The canal was dug through the isthmus at sea level and has no locks. It is 4 miles long and only 70 foot wide at its base, making it impassable for many modern ships. It has little economic importance and is mainly a tourist attraction.

 

The canal was initially proposed in classical times and a failed effort was made to build it in the 1st century AD. The emperor Nero was the first to attempt to construct the canal, personally breaking the ground with a pickaxe and removing the first basket-load of soil in 67 AD, but the project was abandoned when he died shortly afterwards.

Construction recommenced in 1881 but was hampered by geological and financial problems that bankrupted the original builders. It was completed in 1893, but, due to the canal's narrowness, navigational problems, and periodic closures to repair landslides from its steep walls, it failed to attract the level of traffic expected by its operators.

We stopped briefly at Ancient Corinth, a city-state of antiquity. The site was occupied from before 3000 BC. In 146 BC, Corinth was captured and was completely destroyed by the Roman army. As a newly rebuilt Roman colony in 44 BC, Corinth flourished and became the administrative capital of the Roman province of Achaea. In 1928, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake devastated the new city, which was then rebuilt on the same site. In 1933, there was a great fire, and the new city was rebuilt again.

Driving on down past Kalámata we then arrived at our accommodation at Koróni.

The accommodation was fairly basic (terrible plumbing - "no paper down the toilet"!) but it had, as a consolation, some bizarre mushroom lights in the garden.

 

 

Next morning into the pretty harbour village of Methoni to buy supplies and explore.

 

The Castle of Methoni is a well-preserved medieval fortification which was occupied by the Venetians in c.1700. They added to the ancient battlements and it is still possible to see various Venetian emblems including the winged lion of St. Mark and inscriptions.
 
Then onto Pylos via a wonderful wildlife reserve which included an area of brightly-coloured heathers and other vegetation. The ground underneath was wet and peaty and we had to make our way around it on boardwalks.
We then drove into the small town of Pylos and were surprised to see a Russian naval vessel moored up there. It transpired that it was there to celebrate the Battle of Navarino which took place on 20 October 1827, during the Greek War of Independence (1821–32), in Navarino Bay (modern Pylos). Allied forces from Britain, France, and Russia decisively defeated Ottoman and Egyptian forces which were trying to suppress the Greeks. Celebrations are held every year on 20 October (the day we turned up) with delegates from each of the navies. Britain was represented by a submarine which was moored in the Bay.
