13 - What Story Is That, Charlie?

Twin Peaks recap: episode 13 – a welcome jolt of clarity!

There's a suprising amount of plot work in this episode - but beyond arm-wrestles, congas, Audrey's unusual turn and dodgy deals, there's an overriding atmosphere of loneliness.

'Love doesn't always turn a profit…'

For a series as willingly glacial as Twin Peaks, there's a surprising amount of plot work in Part 13. It's a welcome jolt of clarity after a run of episodes so deliberately oblique that they were starting to risk veering to the interminable. Things feel back on track to an extent. But as we all know, plot work was never really the point with this show. Lynch knows this, and it's what allows him a moment like when the Las Vegas cops basically crack the Dale Cooper case and then throw the findings in the bin, because it's all just all too ludicrous, right?

So I'm not particularly interested in the details of Ray's betrayal of Evil Coop and his orders from Phillip Jeffries and the arm-wrestle-stand-off - enjoyably bizarre though it is. Or of Anthony's betrayal of Coop and the agency and his deal with Duncan Todd and the bent cops, even though Tom Sizemore's overacting is always a delight. And let's not even try and rationalise the Red Room. All of this allows Kyle MacLachlan ample space for his elegantly understated performances in the dual roles ahead of awards season (seriously, shower the man with gongs).

But of course, Twin Peaks' most valuable currencies are in atmosphere, mythology, quiet moments and symbolism - and Part 13 had those in incredible golden shit shovels. Damn Fine Cherry Pie, being the most iconic of this show's symbols combined with Damn Fine Coffee. And a mix of both affords Dougie-Coop some rejuvenation beyond his near miss with a panicked and murderous Anthony. There is something of Dale Cooper rising within the man who spends his days bumping into glass doors and repeating the end of other people's sentences.

As it should be, here is the elixir, and Dougie is instinctively seeking it out as Cooper's way back. Elsewhere, the mere mention of the pie from her mother puts a smile back on Bratty Becky's face. But it's not working for Coop because, as we also discover, the other branches of the RR Diner are serving an inferior version of Norma's prized recipe, so the pie is not having the effect. You can practically hear Lynch baiting studio executives with Walter's speech about "modifying a product to ensure maximum profitability."

This quiet Diner sequence also opens up another theme of the week; of loneliness. We get the return of Big Ed Hurley, sat literally opposite Norma, but the pair remain oceans apart, neither especially happy. That closing sequence of Big Ed eating soup alone in silence is unbearably desolate. As is that of Sarah Palmer, in a pit of empty vodka bottles and cigarette butts, watching a boxing clip on loop, waiting for death. The years between (the years without Agent Cooper?) have not been kind to the residents of Twin Peaks. But for one resident in particular, things look especially bleak.

'Are you gonna stop playing games, or do I have to end your story too?'

This week's top theory doing the rounds: Charlie is not in fact Audrey's husband but her therapist. Which doesn't exactly exonerate his bedside manner either, but still. Clearing up confusion (mine at least) about chronology here, timeline-wise, events this week took place pretty much in tandem with last week. Picking things right up, Charlie is in no hurry to reveal to Audrey the details of his mysterious phone call with the mysterious Tina. Turning on a pin from the argumentative banshee from just moments before, here Audrey is a scared little girl, now panicking at the prospect of venturing to the Roadhouse alone and crumpling at the mention of 'stories'. It speaks to a patient, but whatever is going on here, something is clearly very wrong. Is Audrey suffering a split personality? Has some terrible trauma reduced her to a childlike state? Whatever's going on, Sherilyn Fenn's belated return has given The Return another welcome jolt, and I'm terrified about what's going to be revealed.

Talking of the Hornes, wayward Richard is revealed to be hiding out with Renzo's band of thugs, staring malevolently at Evil Coop through the CCTV. Does he know that this man is very likely his father?

Peaks and troughs

  • No Gordon, Albert or Diane this week, which robbed us of the Deputy Director's zingers, and I for one am still upset.
  • There's an insightful piece over at Vox about how this show has turned into a proxy about the act of watching television itself.
  • Not for the first time, we hear about the manipulating hand of FBI man Phillip Jeffries, played in the day by David Bowie. Making us mourn him all over again, it's understood that he was supposed to return, but passed away before he was able to film his scenes. Shooting began in 2015, so it's possible to dream that there was just enough time to pull it off and it's been kept secret. But no, it didn't happen.
  • Can we just take a moment to appreciate the Mitchum Brothers' conga?
  • It's not clear if there's any point to the Dr Amp subplot other than to give Eyepatch Nadine something to do, but it's turning into a welcome dose of warmth anyway.
  • Despite Big Ed's tragic closing sequence, we did still have ourselves a trip to Bang Bang, and the soundtrack this week brought something a bit special. The house performer is none other than heritage character James Hurley (Laura Palmer's secret boyfriend) played again by actor James Marshall, performing the Lynch/Badalamenti-penned lilting waltz Just You - a callback to the very beginning.
