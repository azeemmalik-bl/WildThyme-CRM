Rovinj - July 2009

Julian and I went off on the bus one day to Porec to visit the Euphrasian Basilica - built and dedicated to Mary in the sixth century during the period of Bishop Euphrasius. It was built from 553 on the site of the older basilica that had become dilapidated. For the construction, parts of the former church were used and the marble blocks were imported from the coast of the Sea of Marmara. The wall mosaics were executed by Byzantian masters. The construction took about ten years.

Inside, the two aisles are separated from the nave by 18 elegant Greek marble colonnades with richly sculpted Byzantine and Romanesque capitals, decorated with depictions of animals. They all carry the monogram of Saint Euphrasius. The arches between the capitals are decorated with stucco work.

The most striking feature of the basilica are its mosaics which date from the 6th century, and which are thought to be by the same craftsmen who created the mosaics at Ravenna. The mosaics in the triumphal arch over the apse represent Christ; holding an opened book with the Latin text "Ego sum lux vera" ("I am the true light") with the Apostles, each with their attribute.

The apse is dominated by the marble ciborium, modelled after the one in St. Mark's in Venice, it was built in 1277 on the orders of Otto, Bishop of Porec. The canopy, decorated with mosaics, is carried by four marble columns that belonged to the previous 6th-century ciborium.

 

We hired a car one day, and set off into the Croatian 'hinterland'. Our first stop was at Buje, known as the "sentinel of Istria" for its hilltop site, and home to the Church of Mater Misericordiae (The Mother of Sorrows).

 
Then onto Groznjan, known as the "town of artists" which has about 20 art galleries, hosts a summer film school and has become an international centre of Croatian music. Groznjan also has an annual jazz festival attracting international musicians, such as Georgie Fame in 2007.
Then to Hum where we climbed an extremely steep hill but found a wonderful restaurant for lunch with a spectacular view.
 
And, finally, to Buzet before returning back to Rovinj.
 
Our final jaunt was a day trip to Pula where we visited the Roman Amphitheatre. On our way we stopped off in the town of Vodnjan where we saw the very Venetian-looking Town Hall in People's Square (below). Many old native residents speak an Istriot language they call Bumbaro. These inhabitants are called the Bumbari.
The Church of St. Biagio (Saint Blaise), the biggest church in Istria is built on remnants of an early Romanesque church that was torn down in 1781.
 
It also houses a sacred art collection which specialises in numerous relics and the mummified bodies of saints, reliquaries from Murano, habits of the saints and valuable old books. We paid our money, were taken behind a screen and then the curator lifted a curtain to show a macabre display of skeletons and mummified saintly bodies.
And then to Pula itself. We chuckled at the names on a door buzzer next to where we parked the car, before we walked into the centre where we saw the Temple of Augustus which was dedicated to the first Roman emperor, Augustus, and probably built during the emperor's lifetime at some point between 27 BC and his death in AD 14.
 
The Arch of the Sergii is an Ancient Roman triumphal arch which commemorates three members of the Sergii family, specifically Lucius Sergius Lepidus, a tribune serving in the twenty-ninth legion that participated in the Battle of Actium and disbanded in 27 BC. This suggests an approximate date of construction: 29–27 BC. The arch stood behind the original naval gate of the early Roman colony. The Sergii were a powerful family of officials in the colony and retained their power for centuries.
The Pula Arena is a Roman amphitheatre which is the only remaining Roman amphitheatre to have four side towers entirely preserved. It was constructed between 27 BC and AD 68, and is among the world's six largest surviving Roman arenas. When we visited, the arena was being used for a music and film festival (Elton John had performed live on 8 July 2009) so the centre was full of temporary seating.
The arena has also been used for cinematic works such as Titus, a 1999 film adaptation of Shakespeare's tragedy Titus Andronicus by Julie Taymor. Astonishingly, two professional ice hockey games were also played there in September 2012.
As with many Roman buildings, it provided work for out-of-work actors to dress up and pretend to be Roman centurions or serving maids etc.
The arena is open to the public daily, and the underground passages below the arena house exhibitions of viticulture and olive growing in Istria.
