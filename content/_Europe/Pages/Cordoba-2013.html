Córdoba - September 2013

One of the first things you see on arriving in Córdoba is the Roman Bridge over the Guadalquivir River which links the area of Campo de la Verdad with Barrio de la Catedral. Built in the early 1st century BC, it was the only bridge in the city for twenty centuries.

 
During the 18th century, numerous statues of The Triumph of Saint Raphael the Archangel were erected to protect the city against the plaque. Of the eight “triumphs” that remain in Córdoba, the most majestic was sculpted by Miguel de Verdiguer and erected near the Roman Bridge in 1765.

 

The most iconic building in Córdoba is the Mezquita. It was built as an Islamic mosque but, before that, the site was host to a Visigothic church, the Catholic Christian Basilica of Saint Vincent of Saragossa. The current building was originally constructed on the orders of Abd al-Rahman I in 785 AD, when Córdoba was the capital of the Muslim-controlled region of Al-Andalus. The mosque was converted to a cathedral in 1236 when Córdoba was captured by the Christian forces of Castile during the Reconquista. It has many notable metal exterior doors which add lustre to an already mesmerising building.

 

The Mezquita's Hypostyle hall dates from the original mosque construction and originally served as its main prayer space for Muslims. It also would have served as a hall for teaching and for Sharia law cases during the rule of Abd al-Rahman and his successors. The famous alternating red and white horseshoe arches were inspired by those in the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and also resemble those of Aachen Cathedral, which were built almost at the same time.

   

 
There is a museum incorporated into the Mezquita (Museo de San Clemente) which features all sorts of religious knick-knackery which I love, including a Roman Missal which was published in Antwerp in 1634.

 

 
It is an astonishing building with its crazy mix of Islamic and high Gothic architecture.
 

   

The Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos (Castle of the Christian Monarchs) is located in the historic centre of Córdoba near the Grand Mosque. The fortress served as one of the primary residences of Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon. Its construction was ordered by King Alfonso XI of Castile in 1328, on previous constructions (the Islamic-era Umayyad Alcázar, also the previous residence of the Roman Governor and the Customs).

 
It later served as home to the first permanent tribunal of the Spanish Inquisition and as a headquarters for their campaign against the Nasrid dynasty in Granada, the last remaining Moorish kingdom in the Iberian Peninsula. The Inquisition began using the Alcázar as one of its headquarters in 1482, converting much of it, including the Arab baths, into torture and interrogation chambers. The Inquisition maintained a tribunal here for three centuries.
 

Gardens have existed at the Alcázar since the 10th century when Abd al-Rahman III constructed an aquaduct to bring water from the nearby Albolafia waterwheel. The gardens have been neglected or renewed at various times since then. In the late 15th century Queen Isabella I ordered the waterwheel's operation to be halted, and they went into a general decline. Today the gardens consist of an impressive area of approximately 55,000m² of magnificent gardens with forest species including palm, cypress, orange and lemon trees, which surround a number of elegant fountains and ponds.

The Lower Garden also includes the Paseo de los Reyes (King's Avenue or Promenade of Kings), an avenue bordered by lines of cylindrical cypress trees and divided into two paths by two narrow ponds. On this walk, there are several sculptures of the Kings who built the Alcázar, which are located on pedestals aligned between the hedges.
 
The journey back to Mijas was hot and the roads were fairly punishing, so we stopped for refreshment at a service station and enjoyed a coffee and flan - Andalusia's version of crème caramel.
