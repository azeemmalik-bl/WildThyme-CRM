San Cristóbal
We knew we would have a busy day and, as our hotel served terrible coffee, we went out to find a decent cup before we had to face another day sightseeing.
 
Downtown San Cristóbal with shots of local inhabitants around the Cathedral, currently cordoned off with corrugated iron panels due to the devastating earthquake in 2017 which killed 98 people.
 
 
 
 
   
 
 
Located just north of the centre of town, the imposing 16th century Templo de Santo Domingo is San Cristóbal's most beautiful church. The baroque frontage, with outstanding filigree stucco work, was added in the 17th century and includes the double-headed Hapsburg eagle, then the symbol of the Spanish monarchy.
Also located in this 16th century Dominican convent is the Museo de los Altos de Chiapas, which has impressive archeological relics including these extraordinary pre-Colombian masks made from charred wood and real teeth.
 
 
 
 
There are major exhibits on the Spanish conquest of the 1520s and evangelisation of the region
 
  
 
   
The convent also houses the Centro de Textiles del Mundo Maya. Exquisite embroidery, weaving, needlework and chicken feathers (I kid you not) plus the homespun tunics in thick black wool which are worn by the Chamulan people.
 
 
 
  

Interesting to see that the ofrenda (Spanish: "offering" - a collection of objects placed on a ritual display) was still in place even several days after the Día de Muertos celebration, as here in San Cristóbal. Aztec culture considered souls to live and enter different realms continuously when a body dies - a mixture of Aztec and Christian beliefs that the soul is eternal (whether it be in heaven, purgatory or hell) - and which was propogated during the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire when the two cultures were merged. The ofrenda exists in order to commemorate the souls of loved ones in the family - even in a museum setting.
