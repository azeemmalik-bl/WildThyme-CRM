Palenque

Swathed in morning jungle mists and echoing to a chorus of howler monkeys and parrots, the mighty Maya temples of Palenque are deservedly one of the top destinations of Chiapas and one of the best examples of Maya architecture in all of Mexico.

It was already hot and humid when we arrived at 9am hoping to avoid the crowds.

 
 
 
The name Palenque (Palisade) is Spanish and has no relation to the city's ancient name, which may have been Lakamha (Big Water). Palenque was first occupied around 100 BC and flourished from around AD 630 to 740 and rose to prominence under the ruler Pakal, who reigned from AD 615 to 683. He lived to the incredible age of 80.
 
 
 
The tomb of Pakal is inside the Templo de la Inscripciones (Temple of the Inscriptions) and is perhaps the most celebrated burial monument in the Americas. It is constructed on eight levels and has a central front staircase which rises 25m to a series of small rooms. From the top, interior stairs lead down into Pakal's tomb but this is now closed to visitors to avoid further damage to the murals caused by humidity from visitors' breath.  A replica of the tomb is on show at the Anthropological Museum in Mexico City - along with Pakal's jade death mask.
 
Diagonally opposite the Templo de las Inscripciones is El Palacio (The Palace), a large structure divided into four main courtyards with a maze of corridors and rooms. Built over 400 years from the 5th century on, it was probably the residence of Palenque's rulers. The structure features a tower which was built in the 8th century and it is believed that it was constructed so that Maya royalty and priests could observe the sun falling directly into the Templo de las Inscripciones during the winter solstice.
The north-eastern courtyard, the Patio de los Cautivos (Patio of the Captives) contains a collection of relief sculptures that seem disproportionately large for their setting; the theory is that they represent conquered rulers and were brought from elsewhere.
 
 
 
On the Templo de la Cruz Folliada (Temple of the Foliated Cross), the corbel arches are fully exposed, revealing how Palenque's architects designed these buildings. The 'cross' carvings in some buildings here symbolise the ceiba tree which, in Maya belief, held up the universe.
 
The Templo del Sol (Temple of the Sun) has the best preserved roofcomb at Palenque.
 

Steeps steps climb to the Templo de la Cruz (Temple of the Cross), the largest and most elegantly proportioned in this group. 

 
The Temple of the Count was named after Jean-Frédéric Waldeck (1766-1875) an eccentric scoundrel who lived for two years inside the temple with his Mayan mistress. He was a man of talent and accomplishment, but his love of self-promotion and refusal to let the truth get in the way of a good story leave some aspects of his life in mystery. The temple is a classic Palenque Mayan pyramid with a tall stepped pyramid with central stairwell leading up to the temple at the top. On the top it is adorned with a richly decorated roof comb. Constructed between AD 640 - 650, it is the oldest excavated building so far. Three tombs with a number of human bodies and sacrificial offering were found underneath the portico’s floor.

On the steps leading up to this Temple was a large iguana who appeared to be enjoying the sun but didn't seem to be too fazed by us, although we didn't get too close ...

 

While the Templo de la Calavera (Temple of the Skull) sounds deliciously cadaverous, the truth is much more prosaic and the skull merely refers to a relief sculpture of a rabbit or deer skull which is found at the base of one of its pillars.

 

At the exit to the site a woman was displaying this tunic made from tree bark which was "used by the Lacandónes". It was unclear whether it was for sale or just for exhibition but the jewellry round the neck was spectacular.
