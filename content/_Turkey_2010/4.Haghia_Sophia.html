Istanbul - Hagia Sophia

.

Designed as an earthly mirror of the heavens, the interior of Hagia Sophia succeeds in imparting a truly celestial feel.

The 'church of holy wisdom', Hagia Sophia is among the world's greatest achievements. More than 1,400 years old, it stands as a testament to the sophistication of the 6th century Byzantine capital. The vast edifice was built over two earlier churches and inaugurated by Emperor Justinian in 537. In the 15th century the Ottomans converted it into a mosque. 

 

The Sultan's loge (below and left), was built by the Fossati Brothers, Italian-Swiss architcts who undertook a major restoration of the Hagia Sophia for Sultan Abdül Mecit in 1847-49. 

Next to the loge, is the minber (pulpit) which was installed by Murat III (1574-95).

 

The mihrab (below), the niche indicating the direction of Mecca, was installed in the apse of the church directly opposite the entrance. 

 

 

In the north-western and western corners of the church are two enormous marble urns which are thought to date from the Hellenistic or early Byzantine period. 

A rectangular pillar behind one of the urns, the Pillar of St.Gregory the Miracle-Worker, is believed to have healing or supernatural powers if you touch the moisture which is said to emanate from it. 

   

The church has a series of magnificent mosaics, the first of which were completed during the reign of Justin II.  Most of the mosaics were created in the 10th and 12th centuries, following the periods of Byzantine Iconoclasm. During the Sack of Constantinople in 1204, the Latin Crusaders vandalised valuable items in every important Byzantine structure of the city, including these golden mosaics. Many of these items were shipped to Venice, whose Doge had organised the invasion and sack of Constantinople.

Following the building's conversion into a mosque in 1453, many of its mosaics were covered with plaster, due to Islam's ban on representational imagery. This process was not completed at once, and reports exist from the 17th century in which travellers note that they could still see Christian images in the former church. Above and below is a striking mosaic of the Archangel Gabriel. 

The most complete mosaic is that of the Virgin with Constantine and Justinian (below). Mary sits on a throne holding the infant Jesus and is flanked by two of the greatest emperors of the city. This mosaic was probably created in the 10th century, long after either of their reigns.
Another shows the Virgin with Emperor John Comnenus and Empress Irene.
There is a serene image of Christ which features in the so-called Deësis Mosaic, showing the Virgin Mary and John the Baptist with Christ Pantocractor (the All-Powerful).
There is another mosaic of Christ with Emperor Constantine IX Monomachus and Empress Zoe. The faces of the emperor and empress have been altered.
After lunch we visited the Çemberlitas Baths, a splendid haman complex where I experienced a most invigorating massage. The Baths were conveniently next door to Constantine's Column which was constructed in 330 as part of the celebrations to inaugurate the new Byzantine capital. It is made from porphyry and was brought from Heliopolis in Egypt. Originally it was topped by a capital bearing a statue of Emperor Constantine dressed as Apollo. It was brought down in a storm in 1106 and damaged by a major fire in 1779 (which decimated the Grand Bazaar) but, each time, has been resurrected.
