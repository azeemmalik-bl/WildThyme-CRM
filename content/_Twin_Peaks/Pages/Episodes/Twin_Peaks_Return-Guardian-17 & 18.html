Twin Peaks finale recap – something very special happened here

Sure, David Lynch gave us no closure and it went on far too long. But The Return has undeniably proved one of the most electrifying TV events in years.

Agent Cooper will not let things lie … Twin Peaks.

A few thoughts before we get to the meat and the WTF of the thing. I feel as conflicted by this experience as most of the characters seem to. By far the most delicious morsels of The Return have been the quieter, less plot-related moments: Log Lady’s phone calls; Big Ed and Norma’s slow-burn love affair; anything with Dr Amp or the Mitchum brothers; Gordon Cole’s shouty cadence; Dougie’s complete failure to play catch with Sonny Jim. These moments made this series – and yet, next to none of them had anything much to do with the story being told, though it would have been far poorer without them.

But … did it have to go on for so long? Barely anything happened over 18 hours. I’m being contrary here – as contrary as this show deserves. Because if you sign up for David Lynch, you deserve all you get. That said, few would deny that this has proved one of the most electrifying TV events in years. So let’s get down to business.

‘Once we cross, it could all be different’

Back within city limits … it’s Agent Cooper – and the coffee’s on for him.

I have used the word “coalesce” far too frequently over the course of this series, either out of frustration that it wasn’t happening, celebration that it was or sorrow when it did too much. It’s not easy being a Peaks fan.

Entirely predictably, this two-part finale gives with one hand, takes away with another, then smacks you around the cranium with a super-powered green rubber fist. David Lynch and Mark Frost briefly allow us a climax, as Agent Cooper arrives back in Twin Peaks and for a moment things … coalesce.

Climax reached, bad guy defeated, right? Except we’re barely 30 minutes in …

Gordon Cole throws a special exposition party for Albert and Tammy. Dale passes Twin Peaks city limits eager for the coffee to be on, alerting the Sheriff’s office to the fact that their visitor is an impostor (his refusal of coffee should have been clue enough). Then Lucy shoots Mr C dead, the Woodsmen summon the Bob orbs from his stomach, Freddie gets to play the hero, casting Bob down to hell with his incredible green fist, and Naido is revealed as an avatar for Diane – the real Diane, all scarlet hair and dressing gown.

Climax reached, bad guy defeated, all is well with the world. Right? Except we’re barely 30 minutes in, and there’s business to be taken care of in room 315. Because this is Agent Cooper, and he will not let things lie.

‘It’s difficult to explain. As strange as it sounds, I think you’re a girl called Laura Palmer’

Having been granted our big climax, things take a turn for the baffling.

And so, for reasons best known to David Lynch, Cooper remains compelled to (somehow) try and save Laura Palmer. Having been granted our big climax, things take a turn for the baffling. Cooper and Real-Diane are revealed to be in love, travel 430 miles away, enjoy a hookup in a seedy motel that may or may not trigger troubling memories for Diane depending on what she can still remember. The lady vanishes, Cooper wakes up in an entirely different motel, follows the coordinates to a diner called Judy’s, which is linked somehow to the ancient evil that is Jowday. Another shoot-out ensues, and Cooper disarms some hoodlums by deep-frying their shotguns (obviously). By this point, the old charismatic Dale is fading too – into some beige amalgam of himself and Dougie and Mr C. And then … well, that’s all to discuss below the line. But I believe Lynch and Frost get away with withholding answers because I’ve never witnessed a piece of television so confident in its own skin.

The point is, we were never supposed to understand. Life doesn’t tie up. Sarah Palmer is left, at some unclear point in time, smashing up a photograph of her murdered daughter. No explanation of Audrey’s situation is offered to us, no resolution granted to her. Whatever the true circumstances of that final journey, it’s heavily intimated by Laura/Carrie’s bloodcurdling scream that Agent Cooper’s noble efforts are effectively futile – he can solve a crime, but he can’t heal the wounds it inflicted.


He can solve a crime, but he can’t heal the wounds it inflicted … are all of Agent Cooper’s efforts effectively futile?

All of this has happened before, and all of it will happen again. I think that’s Lynch’s point here. Bad things will occur, lessons will often not be learned, and wondering too much will send you down gigantic rabbit holes. All we can do is try and be the best selves we can. For all of Twin Peaks’ weirdness, it’s actually very simple. It’s the little things that matter – comforting beverages, baked goods, and the small moments of intimacy that, if we’re lucky, we can find with one another. Frustrating as this series has been, something special happened here.

Peaks and troughs

• Gordon Cole’s final zinger of the week: “Phillip Jeffries, who doesn’t really exist any more.”

• Things at least ended well for the Jones family, with a new, made-to-order Dougie delivered right on time.

• You can see why Sherilyn Fenn was dissatisfied with her storyline. The Horne family seemed entirely extraneous throughout. EW’s podcast suggested an idea of their downfall being some comment on the decline of the all-American family. That good idea was still tossed away, I feel.

• What of Norma? Big Ed? Shelley? Becky?

• That said, I’m sticking with the theory that Billy was the gammy-mouthed guy in the jail cell.

• This surely must be the end – even without the narrative (lack of) closure. So on a personal note, thank you all on here for your engagement – tough crowd though you are!

• Nevertheless … “I hope to see you all again. Every one of you.” Now here’s Julee Cruise to play us out.
