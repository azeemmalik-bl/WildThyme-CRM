Venice - April 2010
     
 
 

After 31 years, it was time to return to Venice - this time to celebrate my 50th birthday.

We took a vaporetto (water bus) down the Grand Canal to reaquaint ourselves with the layout of Venice
The Rialto Bridge is the oldest of the four bridges spanning the Grand Canal. Connecting the sestieri (districts) of San Marco and San Polo, it has been rebuilt several times since its first construction as a pontoon bridge in 1173. The present stone bridge, a single span designed by Antonio da Ponte, was built between 1588 and 1591. On either side of the portico, the covered ramps carry rows of shops. The engineering of the bridge was considered so audacious that architect Vincenzo Scamozzi predicted future ruin. The bridge has defied its critics to become one of the architectural icons of Venice.

At Saint Mark's Square, we took another vaporetto out to the Lido.

 
Back then to the water around Saint Mark's Square to the vaporetto station and then a visit to the Doge's Palace.
The Doge's Palace is built in Venetian Gothic style and was the residence of the Doge of Venice, the supreme authority of the former Republic. It was built in 1340, extended and modified in the following centuries and it became a museum in 1923. When we visited part of the façade was covered up due to restoration work.
 
In 1485, the Great Council of Venice decided that a ceremonial staircase should be built within the Palace courtyard. Since 1567, the Giants’ Staircase has been guarded by Sansovino's two colossal statues of Mars and Neptune, which represent Venice’s power by land and by sea, and the reason for its name. Members of the Senate gathered before government meetings in the Senators' Courtyard, to the right of the Giants’ Staircase.
 
The Bridge of Sighs (Ponte dei Sospiri) connects the New Prison (Prigioni Nuove) to the interrogation rooms in the Doge's Palace. It was designed and built in 1600 by Antonio Contino, whose uncle Antonio da Ponte designed the Rialto Bridge. The view from the Bridge of Sighs was allegedly the last view of Venice that convicts saw before their imprisonment. The bridge's English name was bequeathed by Lord Byron in the 19th century as a translation from the Italian from the suggestion that prisoners would sigh at their final view of beautiful Venice through the window before being taken down to their cells. A trip out to the islands on the Venetian Lagoon. The first stop was at the Isola di San Michele, Venice's cemetery. Along with the neighbouring island of San Cristoforo, it was selected to become a cemetery in 1807 when, under French occupation, it was decreed that burial on the main Venetian islands was unsanitary. The canal that separated the two islands was filled in during 1836, and subsequently the larger island became known as San Michele. Bodies were carried to the island on special funeral gondolas. Among those buried there are Igor Stravinsky, Sergei Diaghilev and Ezra Pound. The cemetery is still in use today although disappointingly closed on the day when we tried to visit.
The next stop, Murano, was made up of seven islands, linked by bridges over eight channels. It has a reputation as the centre for glassmaking. In the 13th century, many of Venice’s buildings were made of wood. Glass factories at this time would often catch fire and the Venetian Republic feared the devastation of their city and ordered all glass furnaces to be moved to the nearby island of Murano. It is now a tourist trap and we didn't delay here too long.
Burano is known for its small, brightly painted houses, which follow a specific system. The idea was so that fisherman in the village would recognise their houses in the fog, which is prevalent during early hours much of the year. The island is also known for lacemaking..
 
And finally to Torcello. In pre-Medieval times, Torcello was a much more powerful trading centre than Venice. Thanks to the lagoon’s saline marshes, salt became Torcello’s economic backbone and its harbour developed quickly into an important re-export market. However the Black Death devastated the area of Venice and all the nearby islands, and Torcello was a bigger problem due to the swamp area that completely surrounds the island; malaria was greatly aggravated as well. Navigation in the laguna morta (dead lagoon) was impossible before long and traders ceased coming. As a result, the population dwindled and even the bishop transferred to Murano, leaving only a few people behind..
 
Torcello was the bishop’s official seat for more than a thousand years. Visitors brought with them the relics of Saint Heliodorus, now the patron saint of the island. It was first settled in the year 452 and has been referred to as the parent island from which Venice was populated.
Locanda Cipriani is still owned by the family who run Harry's Bar. Simple but world-famous it was where we came for my 50th birthday lunch which was extremely memorable.
 
San Giorgio Maggiore is one of the islands of Venice, lying south of the main island group. The island is now best known for the Church of San Giorgio Maggiore, designed by Palladio and begun in 1566. The belltower has a ring of 9 bells in C#. In the early 19th century, after the Republic fell, the island became a free port with a new harbour built in 1812. It became the home of Venice's artillery. It has been much painted, featuring for example in a series by Monet and also contains several paintings by, amongst others, Jacopo Tintoretto.

We climbed the campanile (bell tower) of the church and enjoyed glorious views over the whole of Venice and the lagoon.
The large bell started ringing while we were up the tower. It was very loud!
Santa Maria della Salute, commonly known simply as the Salute, is a minor basilica which stands on the narrow finger of Punta della Dogana, between the Grand Canal and the Giudecca Canal. In 1630, Venice experienced an unusually devastating outbreak of the plague. As a votive offering for deliverance from the pestilence, the Republic of Venice vowed to build and dedicate a church to Our Lady of Health. Construction began in 1631. Many of the objects of art housed in the church bear references to the Black Death.
Back in Saint Mark's Square, the crowds were diminishing at the end of a busy day, so it was now possible to admire the façade of the Doge's Palace and to see the famous Venetian winged lions.
The Basilica di Santa Maria Gloriosa dei Frari, usually just called the Frari, is one of the most prominent churches in the city. It has the status of a minor basilica and is dedicated to the Assumption of Mary. The interior contains a Bellini altarpiece and works by Titian and Donatello and is notable for many grand monuments to distinguished Venetians who are buried in the church, including the sculptor Canova, the composer Monteverdi and the painter Titian.

Venice has its own opera house - the Teatro La Fenice.
We spent the rest of the afternoon getting lost in the muddle of walkways, bridges and canals.
 
We were amused to see several gondoliers having a sleep in their gondolas.
 

The Chiesa di San Moisè was built initially in the 8th century. It is dedicated to Moses since like the Byzantines, the Venetians often considered Old Testament prophets as canonised saints. It also honours Moisè Venier, the aristocrat who funded the reconstruction. The elaborate Baroque facade, dating from 1668, is profusely decorated with sculpture, including a bust of its patron, Vincenzo Fini, which is found over the entry door. Statues in public spaces were forbidden in Venice, but by putting his bust on the façade of a church, he could circumvent this ordinance and display his wealth and his recent addition to the Venetian aristocracy.
