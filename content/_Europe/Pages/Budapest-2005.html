Budapest - February 2005
     
 
 

A trip to Hungary to visit my American friend Tom Rooney (with whom I stayed in San Francisco back in 1991). Tom had moved to Hungary to do his Peace Corps volunteering and had met and married György, an English teacher who specialised in the works of Jane Austen.

Hösök tere (Heroes' Square) is one of the major sites in Budapest, noted for its iconic statue complex featuring the Seven chieftains of the Magyars and other Hungarian national leaders, as well as the Memorial Stone of Heroes. We then walked across the Chain Bridge, one of Budapest's important river crossings across the Danube.
 

Posing in front of the River Danube.

On our trip on the Budapest metro we were amused to see the Hungarian poster for Wesley Snipes' film Blade: Trilogy ...
The Hotel Gellért features the famous bath complex which was built between 1912-1918 in the (Secession) Art Nouveau style. References to healing waters in this location are found from as early as the 13th century. A hospital was located on this site during the Middle Ages. During the reign of the Ottoman Empire, baths were also built on this site. The "magical healing spring" was used by the Turkish during the 16th and 17th centuries. The bath was called Sárosfürdö ("muddy" bath) because the mineral mud settled at the bottom of pools.

The Gellért Baths feature elaborately-tiled steam rooms and plunge pools - as well as a covered swimming pool. We decided to "take the plunge" (well, it would be churlish not to) and found the water deliciously warm.
After having our swim, we climbed the nearby Gellért Hill through the snow to see the statue of Szent Gellért (St. Gerard) who was a bishop canonised after his assassination by pagans during the great pagan rebellion in 1046. He was put in a barrel and thrown from the top of the hill.
On our final evening, we met Tom and György again who took us to a 'typically Hungarian' restaurant but the food, I must admit, I cannot now remember.
St. Stephen’s Basilica is a Roman Catholic church named in honour of Stephen, the first King of Hungary (c.975-1038), whose right hand is housed in the reliquary. We only saw it by night so missed the hand.
