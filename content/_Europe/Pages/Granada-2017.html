Granada - September 2017

Both Seán and I had been to the Alhambra in the past, but never together. As keen gardeners it was a definite destination for us and we weren't disappointed after our long drive from Mijas.

A palace and fortress complex, the Alhambra was originally constructed as a small fortress in 889 AD on the remains of Roman fortifications, and then largely ignored until its ruins were renovated and rebuilt in the mid-13th century by the Nasrid Emir, Mohammed ben Al-Ahmar. It was converted into a royal palace in 1333 by Yusuf I, Sultan of Granada.

After the conclusion of the Christian Reconquista in 1492, the site became the Royal Court of Ferdinand and Isabella (where Christopher Columbus received royal endorsement for his expedition), and the palaces were partially altered in the Renaissance style. In 1526 Charles V commissioned a new Renaissance palace better befitting the Holy Roman Emperor in the revolutionary Mannerist style, in direct juxtaposition with the Nasrid Andalusian architecture, but it was ultimately never completed due to Morisco rebellions in Granada which used up all the funds.

The Palacio de Carlos V was designed in 1527 by Pedro Machuca (who studied under Michelangelo). The builders ran out of money in 1568 and the palace remained without a roof until the early 20th century when work started again. A fabulous atmospheric place, notable as the major dominant Christian building at the Alhambra.

 

Beyond the Palace is the area which was originally a large soukh or public market square. This faces the Alcazaba (see below), but is now subdivided and obscured by later Christian-era development. The Alhambra resembles many medieval Christian strongholds in its threefold arrangement as a castle, a palace and a residential annex for subordinates.

 

The Alhambra's most westerly feature is the Alcazaba (citadel), a strongly fortified position built on the isolated and precipitous foreland which terminates the plateau on the northwest.

There are fabulous views over modern-day Granada to be seen from the citadel.

 

 

  

Pretty cooling fountains in the Jardin de los Adarves (Parapet Garden) of the Alcazaba.
 
 
 
The Mexuar (or Cuarto Dorado) is modest in decor and houses the functional areas for conducting business and administration. It was built by Muhammad V, the eldest son and heir of Yusuf I (by his slave Butayna). Born in 1339, he ruled between 1354–1359 and 1362–1391.
 
 
 
 
The Court of the Myrtles (Patio de los Arrayanes) is part of the Palacio de Comares. Its current name is due to the myrtle bushes that surround the central pool, the bright green colour of which contrasts with the white marble of the patio. The pool helped to cool the palace and acted as a symbol of power: because water was usually in short supply, the technology required to keep these pools full was expensive and difficult.
 
The Court of the Lions (Patio de los Leones) is an oblong courtyard surrounded by a low gallery supported on 124 white marble columns. In the centre of the court is the Fountain of the Lions, an alabaster basin supported by the figures of twelve lions in white marble - symbols of strength, power and sovereignty. Every hour one lion would produce water from its mouth.
 
 
The Sala de los Abencerrajes (Hall of the Abencerrages) derives its name from a legend in which the father of Boabdil, the last sultan of Granada, having invited the chiefs of that line to a banquet, massacred them here. This room is a perfect square, with a lofty roof – a dome honeycombed with tiny cells, all different, and said to number 5000 – is an example of the 'stalactite vaulting' of the Moors.
 
 
The Hamman (Royal Baths) are the only remaining baths of the eight which originally served the 2,000 or so inhabitants of the Alhambra. They were reserved for the sultans’ private use. However, they were an exception since the majority were public and their popularity was linked to pre-prayer body purification rituals. Above we can see the exterior of the roof to the Baths with skylights piercing the roof covering.
The private, internalised universe of the Palacio de Los Leones (Palace of the Lions) adjoins the public spaces. These private areas overlook the refined Garden of Lindaraja and the Patio de Daraxa (Court of the Vestibule).
 
Of the outlying buildings connected to the Alhambra, the foremost in interest is the Palacio de Generalife ("Garden of Arif" or "Garden of the Architect"). The palace and gardens were built during the reign of Muhammed II (1273-1302), Sultan of Granada, and later by Muhammed III (1302–1309). They were redecorated shortly after by Abu I-Walid Isma'il (1313–1324).

 
 
Much of the garden is a recent reconstruction - commenced in 1931 and completed by Francisco Prieto Moreno in 1951. They are not necessarily 'authentic' - even though they are probably the best known part of the site.
