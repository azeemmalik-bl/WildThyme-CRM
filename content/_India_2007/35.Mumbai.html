A taxi to Diu airport and then a flight via Porbandar (birthplace of Mahatma Gandhi) to Mumbai. On our first morning we took a boat trip from the Gateway of India (Mumbai's most famous colonial monument) to Elephanta Island.

  
     

Originally known as Gharapuri (the 'city of Ghara priests'), the island was renamed in the sixteenth century by the Portuguese in honour of the carved elephant they found at the port. On arrival at the island you are met by a dinky train and cool drink and souvenir stalls which line the way up the hill to the caves.

 
   

Elephanta's impressive cave system was built (or, more strictly, dug) in the eighth century. Inside, the massive columns carved from solid rock, give the deceptive impression of being structural.

A powerful 6m-high bust of Trimurti, the three-faced Shiva, who embodies the powers of creator, preserver and destroyer stands near a large lingam which is surrounded by coins and smouldering joss left by devotees.

 
At the entrance to the cave is a panel of Nataraj, Shiva as the cosmic dancer. It was damaged by the Portuguese who, it is said used it for target practice. Shiva's face is rapt and, in one of his left hands, he removes the veil of ignorance.

High on the top of Malabar Hill, screened from prying eyes by an imposing wall and a dense curtain of vegetation, stand the seven Parsi Towers of Silence or dokhmas. The Parsis dispose of their dead by leaving the corpses on top of tall cylindrical enclosures for their bones to be picked clean by vultures. This ancient mortuary ritual was advocated by the prophet Zoroaster as a means of avoiding pollution of the four sacred elements (air, water, earth, and the holiest of them all, fire). We tried to visit the Towers by taxi but, despite almost making it to the top of the hill, we were turned back by the guards.

Near Mahalakshmi Railway Station we saw the municipal dhobi ghats, a huge open-air laundry. Each morning, washing from all over Mumbai is brought here to be thrown into soapy piles and thumped by the resident dhobi wallahs in countless concrete tanks. The next day, after being aired, pressed, folded in newspaper and bound with cotton thread, the bundles are returned to their owners. A bizarre photo-opportunity.
Our last evening in Mumbai found us in a splendid curry house with an Arsenal-supporting waiter who supplied us with cocktails as well as delicious food. A marvellous send off on our way back to the UK.
