Greece - October 2005

We then arrived back into Athens and visited the Hellenic Parliament where we saw the Evzones, a special unit of the Hellenic Army, also known as Tsoliades, who guard the Monument of the Unknown Soldier in front of the Presidential Mansion. The unusual uniform includes the foustanella, a cotton kilt made from 30 metres (98 feet) of cloth, with supposedly 400 pleats representing the 400 years of Ottoman occupation and the tsarouchia, red leather clogs with black foundes (pompons).

  

Near the Greek Parliament was a Greek Orthodox church with ornate chandelier and exquisite jewelled icons - and lots of incense.
 
 

And then onto the Acropolis - Athens' most famous tourist attraction. The Odeon of Herodes Atticus from above.

The most notable part of the Acropolis is the Parthenon - a former temple dedicated to the goddess Athena, whom the people of Athens considered their patron. Construction began in 447 BC when the Athenian Empire was at the peak of its power. It was completed in 438 BC and is the most important surviving building of Classical Greece, generally considered the zenith of the Doric order. Its decorative sculptures are considered some of the high points of Greek art - but many of which were removed by Lord Elgin and later displayed in the British Museum. Others were removed and are now in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin.
 

 

The Erechtheion is a temple on the north side of the Acropolis which was dedicated to both Athena and Poseidon. On the north side, there is another large porch with six Ionic columns, and on the south, the famous 'Porch of the Maidens', with six draped female figures (caryatids) as supporting columns. These were notably copied in St. Pancras New Church on the Euston Road in London - built 1819-22 by William and Henry Inwood.

 

There was much restoration work taking place on the Acropolis site with cranes and other heavy lifting equipment reassembling many collapsed structures.
 

Next to the Acropolis site was a fascinating Archeological Museum which was being constructed and extended while we were there. Not all rooms were open at this time.
 

Leaving the Acropolis site, we went down for lunch and had a drink opposite the structure (below).
 
The Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Plaka, Athens as it is today, and in an engraving from 1840 by W.Richardson. It was erected by Lysicrates, a wealthy patron of musical performances in the Theatre of Dionysus. The monument is known as the first use of the Corinthian order on the exterior of a building. It has been reproduced widely on modern monuments and buildings.
The Zappeion is in the National Gardens of Athens. It was built between 1874 and 1888 specifically for the revival of the Olympic Games in the modern world. Unfortunately its benefactor, Evangelis Zappas, did not live long enough to see the Zappeion built. It is now used for meetings and ceremonies.
On our final day we drove out to Cape Sounion, east of Athens to see the Temple of Poseidon.
 
A previous visitor to the Temple of Poseidon was Lord Byron who engraved his name on one of the columns supporting the roof.
 
And a final lunch next to the sea before returning to the airport and the flight home ...
