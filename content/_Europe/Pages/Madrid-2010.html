Madrid - October 2010
     
 
 

A cultural weekend in Madrid to see the Thyssen-Bornemisza, Prado and Reina Sofia collections and to take in local 'colour' including fan shops and butchery outlets.  But the first stop was to a bar for a well-deserved glass of sherry.

 

The Congreso de los Diputados is the lower house of the Spanish parliament and meets in this rather unprepossessing nineteenth century building (plus modern extension - below). The two bronze lions that guard the entrance are made from a melted-down cannon captured during the African War of 1859-60.
The Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza is one of the most spectacular collections of private art in the world. Assembled by Baron Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza, his son Hans Heinrich and his former beauty-queen wife Carmen, it opened to the public in 1993 and contains pieces by almost every major Western artist since the fourteenth century. Inside I was too astonished by the quality of the artworks on display to remember to take photographs.
   
And then to the Museo del Prado, one of the oldest and greatest collections of art in the world, largely amassed by the Spanish royal family over the last two hundred years. Numerous works by Francisco Goya, the single most extensively represented artist, as well as by Hieronymus Bosch, El Greco, Peter Paul Rubens, Titian, and Diego Velázquez, are some of the highlights of the collection.

Photography was forbidden in the Prado but I managed to sneak a crafty (if out of focus) photo of Albrecht Dürer's Portrait of an Unidentified Man of 1521. I particularly wanted to see Dürer's Self Portrait of 1498 which I had admired since I was a schoolboy. The wardens in the room housing this painting were particularly conspicuous so I have to include a photo of this found on the internet.
Supper that night was in a local brasserie, noisy but the food (prawns, egg and noodles - not quite sure of the Spanish-ness of this) was delicious. Then a walk back to the hotel via the Plaza Mayor featuring the equestrian statue of King Felipe III which dates from 1616.
The next morning included a visit to the Centro de Arte Reina Sofía - an immense exhibition space providing a permanent home for the Spanish collection of modern and contemporary art, inclding the Miró and Picasso legacies.
 
Roy Lichtenstein - Brushstroke (1996)

Superbly displayed and no longer protected by bullet-proof glass and steel girders that once imprisoned it, Picasso's Guernica is a monumental icon of twentieth-century Spanish art and politics which, despite its familiarity, still has the ability to shock. Painted after the bombing of the Basque town of Gernika in April 1937.

After all that excitement, it was then time for lunch at a great bar just down the road where they make their own churros in front of customers and which are not fattening at all ...
