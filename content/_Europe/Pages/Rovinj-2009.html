Rovinj - July 2009

The previous year, in 2008 when we had gone to Egypt, we had flown down over the coast of Croatia and remarked on how beautiful it looked. 2009 found us in Croatia itself to find out first-hand. Our destination was Rovinj (pronounced Ro-VEEN). In Italy, of which it used to be part, it is called Rovigno.

We had booked a second floor apartment overlooking the harbour - with a roof terrace which gave us terrific views and was a beautiful place to sit with a glass of wine in the evening.

After unpacking, we immediately went down into the harbour area to explore and took a passenger ferry out to one of the local islands (Sveti Andrija) for a swim.

One day, after our swim, we returned to find filming taking place by the local TV company with photogenic presenters and featuring local customs, foods, 'peasant dancing' and gymnastics.

Rovinj was endlessly interesting as there were so many hidden aspects to be seen at different times of the day, giving a variety of views under differing light conditions.
 
 

The Church of Saint Euphemia can be seen from virtually every aspect in Rovinj and her statue on the campanile acts as a weather vane as she changes direction depending on the wind. Euphemia was martyed by the Governor of Chalcedon (near Istanbul in Turkey) in AD 304, having been discovered with forty-nine other Christians hiding in a house and worshipping God. She was placed in the arena, where lions were released to kill her, but instead they licked her wounds. It is believed that she finally died of wounds from a wild bear.

Christians from Chalcedon preserved her body until AD 620 when the town was captured by the Persians. The sarcophagus with the body of St. Euphemia was then transferred to Constantinople. In AD 800 the Iconoclasts came to power, and the Christians were forced to remove her relics.

Legend is that her marble sarcophagus came floating in the sea to the coast of Rovinj after a storm. Many people tried to haul the sarcophagus to the Church of Saint George, but no one succeded. Finally, answering St. Euphemia's call, a small boy with two cows managed to haul the sarcophagus up the hill. The people of Rovinj considered it a miracle, and proclaimed St. Euphemia the patron-saint of the town. Her day is celebrated on September 16. Traditionally, on St. Euphemia's Day people eat mutton with sauerkraut (ovca z kapuzom) and fritule an Istrian delicacy.

 

We also took a boat up to Trieste which is only 30km to the north - but is in fact in Italy. The boat stopped at a third country - Slovenia - on the way. Trieste was one of the oldest parts of the Habsburg Monarchy, belonging to it from 1382 until 1918. In the 19th century Trieste became the fourth largest city of the Austro-Hungarian Empire (after Vienna, Budapest and Prague). In the fin de siècle period at the end of the 19th century it emerged as an important hub for literature and music.

The 'Monument to the Fallen Soldiers in the First World War' is in the Parco della Rimembranza.The sculpture was inaugurated before King Victor Emmanuel III and numerous high-ranking fascist officials in 1935.
Preparations were underway for a festival of some kind, but we never did find out exactly what.
After lots of exploring (including seeing the statue of James Joyce - above), we were ready to sit down and enjoy a drink.
