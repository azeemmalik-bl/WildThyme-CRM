Tikal

Initially settled in about 600 BC Tikal, meaning “the place of spirit voices”, can justly be compared with the celebrated ancient cities of Egypt and Greece. Discovered in 1848 and opened to the public in 1955, it had to be closed in 1969 due to the Guatemalan civil war.
 After an introductory talk by our guide, we were taken onto the site, passing this impressive Kapok Tree (Ceiba pentandra) which was sacred to the Mayan people.
 
 All the structures on the site have very prosaic and, frankly, unilluminating names - for example, the first excavated temple we saw was merely called Complex "R" ...
 It was hot and oppressive in the jungle setting and the bellows of the howler monkeys were disconcerting.
 
 We then came to the tallest temple on the Tikal site (and in fact in all of Mesoamerica) which was named Templo IV. It was built in AD 740 and is 70m tall and it was possible to climb to the very top and enjoy a magnificent view over the jungle canopy.
 
   
Now open again, Tikal is a magnificent Mayan site host to coatis (cheeky raccoon-like creatures) plus oscellated turkeys (which look like an explosion in a paint factory), green toucanets, toucans and weaver birds.
 
 
 

The next area for our exploration was called The Lost World, allegedly named as it has different architectural styles from the rest of the buildings at Tikal. This is the imaginitively-titled Temple 5C-49.
 

The Lost World Pyramid (The Grand Pyramid or Structure 5C-54) dominates this area and is the oldest building at Tikal. Below is a view of the restored west face.

The Plaza de los 7 Templos is so-called because it has seven temples on the east of the plaza as well as a triple ball-playing field, which is unique in the whole Mayan area.
 

Our guide was the son of one of the archeologists who had worked on the site so his knowledge was pretty thorough. He was also a good communicator so it made it a little easier to get your head around the history of the complex in which it was very easy to get completely lost.

We stopped in a shelter outside Templo V (the second highest in the complex) as it poured with rain and he gave an inpromptu talk about Mayan myths and beliefs.
 
 
 
 
The Gran Plaza is the most important part of the complex as the buildings there show the most impressive architectural features. Many of them took more than 1,000 years to build.
 
Also known as 'The Big Jaguar', Templo I was built in 700 AD by Governor Jasaw Chan K'awii I whose tomb was discovered inside the temple.
 
Templo II is also known as 'The Mascarones Temple'. Also built by the Governor who built Templo I, the carving above the lintel, according to the guidebook, appears to resemble his wife, although I couldn't see it myself ...
 
The trees were host to all sorts of air plants and other symbiotic and parasitic life forms.
 
 
After a tiring day, what better than a large cocktail and dinner?
